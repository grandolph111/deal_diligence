What DealDiligence.ai is

When companies buy, sell, or merge, lawyers and bankers must read hundreds of dense contracts to find the risks hidden inside them. That review — called due diligence — is slow, expensive, and easy to get wrong.

DealDiligence.ai is a secure workspace where a deal team uploads those contracts and an AI assistant — Claude — reads every page end-to-end. For each document it produces a clean one-page summary: who the parties are, what the key clauses say, a risk score out of 10, and the biggest red flags. It then stitches every document together into a single living summary of the whole deal, answers questions in plain English, and drafts risk memos on request — all while making sure a junior reviewer can only ever see the folders they've been granted.

2×

Living "deal brief" feeds the chat, the task board, and the dashboard

Who uses it

Investment bankers, M&A lawyers, corporate-development teams, private-equity and venture investors, and auditors — anyone who sits on one side of a deal and needs to understand a mountain of paperwork quickly and safely.

The one-line pitch Think of it as a tireless junior analyst that reads every contract overnight, never misses a clause, shows its work with citations, and hands the senior team a ranked list of what to worry about.

The two big ideas

Almost every clever thing in this codebase comes from two simple decisions. Understanding them makes the rest obvious.

① Everything downstream reads a summary, never the raw PDF

A PDF is heavy and expensive for an AI to read. So the system reads each PDF once, very carefully, and turns it into a lightweight text summary called a fact sheet. After that, every other feature — the chat, the dashboard, the risk memos — reads those tidy fact sheets instead of re-reading the original files. That one choice makes the whole platform fast, cheap, and easy to control.

② Access control is just "which summaries can you load?"

Because everything reads summaries, restricting what a person can see becomes simple: you decide which fact sheets get handed to the AI for that person. A junior reviewer limited to the "Financials" folder literally never has the other documents placed in front of the AI on their behalf — so the chat, their dashboard, and their reports can only ever draw on what they're allowed to see.

Why this matters for the business These two ideas are why the product is both cheap to run (a full deal costs single-digit to low-hundreds of dollars in AI fees) and safe to sell to regulated clients (information barriers are enforced by design, not by trust).

A note on the AI itself

The platform uses three "sizes" of Claude, matched to the job — the same way you'd assign work to staff of different seniority. This keeps quality high where it counts and costs low everywhere else.

Stage by stage

1

Upload — the handshake before any AI

When a user adds a file, the browser uploads it directly to secure cloud storage (Amazon S3) using a temporary one-time link. The bytes never pass through our own servers, and the app responds instantly — it doesn't sit and wait for the AI. A background job is kicked off, and the screen simply shows Uploading → Processing → Complete as it progresses.

Why it's built this wayThe expensive AI work never blocks the website. A user can upload 50 files and keep working while they process in the background.

In the code:

modules/documents/documents.service.ts— initiateUpload() hands out the upload link; confirmUpload() starts the background read.
2

Sort it Haiku

Before the careful read, the fast junior AI skims just the first two pages to decide what kind of document this is — a purchase agreement, an NDA, a lease, a financial statement, and so on (nine types in all). The document type is almost always obvious from the cover page, so there's no need to pay to read all 80 pages here.

integrations/claude/classify.ts
3

Pick the right reader — the cost lever

Now the system chooses which "size" of AI does the heavy read, based on length and type. A 3-page NDA goes to the cheap junior; a 200-page credit agreement goes to the senior expert. There's one smart override: term-dense documents like purchase agreements are always bumped up a level, even when short — they're too important to trust to the junior.

This is the dial that controls costReading every document with the most expensive AI would work — it would just cost several times more. Matching the model to the job is what keeps a full deal in the tens of dollars.

4

The careful read Opus

This is the heart of the product. The chosen AI reads the entire PDF and returns a structured fact sheet: an executive summary, the parties involved, the important clauses (organised by a legal-industry standard called CUAD), a risk score out of 10, the top risks, key dates and dollar amounts, and the relationships between them.

Two refinements make the result better and cheaper:

  • The client's playbook is included. If a client has told us their preferred positions and red flags, the risk score measures how far this contract deviates from what they consider acceptable — not just generic risk.
  • The instructions are "cached." The lengthy rulebook the AI follows is the same every time, so the system stores it and reuses it at a 90% discount on repeat reads.

5

The two safety nets — the defining feature

An AI that reads contracts is only useful if you can trust what it says. So before any result is shown, it passes two independent checks:

Net 1 · The quote checker no AI

Plain, deterministic code pulls the real text out of the PDF and confirms every quote the AI used actually appears on the page it claims. If a quote is on a different page, it's flagged. If a quote doesn't appear anywhere, it's marked a likely fabrication. No AI can talk its way past this — it's pure math.

Net 2 · The second opinion Sonnet

A different AI re-reads the original PDF alongside the fact sheet, hunting for made-up quotes, wrong pages, missing clauses, or a risk score that doesn't match the text. Crucially, it can hand back a corrected version, which the system saves in place.

What the client seesEach document ends up marked Verified, Needs review, or Failed — so a human always knows how much to trust it. This "show your work" guarantee is the product's biggest differentiator.

6

Save the result

The finished fact sheet is written to cloud storage, and the key facts are copied into the database so the rest of the app can sort and filter instantly — the risk score lands on the dashboard, the document type becomes a filter, the parties and dates become searchable. The full write-up stays as the durable, human-readable record.

A quiet money-saverEach file is fingerprinted. If the exact same file is uploaded again unchanged, the system recognises it and skips the expensive re-read entirely — re-uploading a file is free.
services/extraction.service.ts— the conductor that runs steps 2–6 in order, with automatic retries if anything fails.

A note on reliability

Steps 2 through 6 are wrapped in a single coordinator that handles the messy realities of working with an outside AI service: if a request is rate-limited, it waits and retries; if something fails, it backs off and tries again up to three times before marking the document as failed (which a user can manually retry). And if no AI is configured at all — for a demo or offline development — the whole flow still runs on canned sample data, so the product is always clickable.

In one sentence

A document comes in, gets sorted, gets read by the right-sized AI, gets checked twice, and comes out the other side as a trustworthy one-page summary with a risk score — all in the background, without anyone waiting at a screen.

Connecting the dots

One document is useful. The real value is understanding the whole deal — and that means tying every document together.

7

The deal brief Sonnet

About 30 seconds after the last upload finishes, a background pass reads every fact sheet in the deal and produces a single, living deal brief: the parties, the key clauses across all documents, the top five risks for the whole deal, the important dates, and how the documents relate. This brief is the one summary the chat, the task board, and the dashboard all read from.

Why the 30-second waitIf someone bulk-uploads 20 files, the system doesn't rebuild the brief 20 times. It waits until the dust settles and rebuilds once — a simple trick that keeps costs down during busy uploads.

It also matches duplicates

"Acme Corp" in one file and "Acme Corporation" in another are recognised as the same company and merged into one master record — so the deal isn't cluttered with phantom duplicate parties. This also powers a visual relationship map for the team.

And it flags outliers

Once there are at least three comparable documents, the AI can spot when one contract's terms deviate from its peers — an unusually long liability cap, an off-market interest rate — and flag it for attention.

The subtle part: many briefs, not one A junior reviewer restricted to certain folders must not see a brief that summarises documents they're not allowed to view. So the system quietly builds a separate brief for each distinct access level in use. Two people with the same folder access share one brief; a restricted reviewer gets their own, built only from their documents. Their chat, dashboard, and reports all draw from that limited brief automatically.
services/reconciliation.service.ts services/deal-brief.service.ts utils/scope-key.ts

One more thoughtful touch: the deal team's hand-written notes inside a brief are preserved word-for-word every time the AI regenerates it — human edits are never overwritten.

Putting it to work

With trustworthy summaries in place, two features turn them into real work product.

8

The AI task board Opus

The platform has a Kanban board — the familiar columns of cards you drag from To-do to Done. Here, a card can carry an instruction ("Assess the change-of-control provisions") and a few attached documents. When a team member drags it into progress, the senior AI reads the deal brief plus those documents and drafts a risk memo. The card then moves itself into the In Review column, where a specialist checks the AI's work.

The workflow intentThe AI drafts; the human approves. The card literally walks itself from "to-do" to "ready for a specialist" — the AI becomes a member of the team, not a black box.

A safety check runs first: if the person isn't allowed to see an attached document's folder, the report is refused — they can't use the AI to peek at restricted files.

modules/tasks/task-ai.service.ts
9

Ask the deal anything Haiku

Inside the data room, anyone on the team can ask questions in plain English — "What's the longest non-compete in these contracts?" The fast junior AI answers from the user's scoped deal brief and returns citations (which document, which page) so the answer is checkable. If a person has been granted no folders yet, the chat politely declines rather than guessing — access is enforced even here.

modules/chat/chat.service.ts

The common thread: notice that both features read the same deal brief and fact sheets — never the raw PDFs. That's the first big idea paying off. Adding a new AI feature means writing a new prompt over the same summaries, not building new plumbing.

Who sees what

M&A deals are full of information barriers — a junior analyst or an outside party may only be cleared for part of the data room. The platform enforces this everywhere, from one central rulebook.

The seniority ladder

Every member of a deal has a role. Owners and admins see everything. More junior members get an explicit list of folders they're allowed into — and granting a folder automatically grants everything inside it.

RoleWhat they can see
Owner / AdminThe entire deal — full access by default
Member / ViewerOnly their granted folders (and any sub-folders inside them)
Super Admin / Company AdminOversight across their organisation's deals
The single chokepointOne function answers the question "what is this person allowed to see?" and every feature — documents, dashboard, chat, AI reports — asks it before doing anything. Access rules live in one place, not scattered across the app, which is exactly what an auditor wants to hear.

Enforced at every door

  • Document lists hide what you can't open
  • The dashboard only totals your folders
  • Chat retrieval is filtered to your scope
  • AI reports refuse out-of-scope attachments

Where it lives

services/scope.service.ts middleware/permissions.ts utils/scope-key.ts
Tying it backRemember big idea #2: because access is "which summaries get loaded," these rules are a simple filter on a list — not a fragile maze of special cases. That's why the security story is strong enough to take to a regulated buyer.

What it costs to run

Because the design reads each PDF only once and reuses cheap summaries everywhere else, the AI cost of a deal is remarkably low.
